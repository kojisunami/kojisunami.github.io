\textbf{Biography}

\noindent
My freshman/sophomore was community college, no math classes beyond linear algebra,
but I was deciphering Clay math millenium problems, and no outstanding record on Putnam.
My undergraduate junior/senior is all about studying abstract algebra(Girillet) preparing Hartshorne or Alan Hatcher, 
and I obtained a strong recommendation, while I didn't take much class of analysis/physics.
My first PhD is top 50-100 in US news math ranking, where there is a solid research group of mirror symmetry,
a huge subject intersecting the work of roughly half of Fields medal recipients,
doing an ambitious PhD requires a strong SOP so to graduate in time.

\noindent
\textbf{Philosophy} \\

\noindent
What I'd value in philosophy is the clarity of problem setting, accessibility to the audience, and extendability by endless curiousity, 
and furthermore the ability to judge what is said and to correct it if needed in case the author is lying or mistaking,
and Darwin, UNIX, and Newton: nothing is perfect, but the evaluation depends on our efforts.
For the case of math, we aim its clarity with unlimited use of dictionary, typically Bourbaki style,
accessibility to general audience or physics or undergraduate math, and inventing future roadmap because imitating legacy is simply boring.
Math is like climbing a mountain, and skilled climber says climb up to the notch rather than climb down if we're lost, 
and abstract nonsense is the apex of the mountain. We have a freedom of interpretion on Grothendieck or Erdos.
